Money Management & Savings Where to keep cash and how to earn more on it — general education, not personalized advice

Not all your money belongs in the stock market. Money you might need soon (an emergency fund, a house down payment next year) is generally kept somewhere safe and easy to access. Money you won't touch for years can afford more risk for more growth — see Get Started Investing.

This page covers the safer, cash-like options — savings accounts, CDs, and Treasury bills — and how they compare to investing in stocks or funds.

Where should your money live?

The general rule: match the account to when you'll need the money. The sooner you might need it, the more it belongs somewhere stable and easy to withdraw from — even if that means a lower return. The further away, the more room there is to take on risk for growth.

  1. Days to weeks away (bills, spending money): a regular checking account.
  2. Months to ~3 years away (emergency fund, a known upcoming expense): a high-yield savings account, CD, or Treasury bill — see below.
  3. 3+ years away (retirement, long-term goals): investing in stocks/funds becomes more reasonable — see Saving vs. investing and Get Started Investing.

High-yield savings accounts (HYSA) FDIC insurance protects deposits at a US bank up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, if the bank fails — your money is protected even in a worst case. Credit unions have an equivalent, NCUA insurance.

A savings account that pays meaningfully more interest than a typical big-bank savings account (which often pays close to nothing) — usually offered by online banks with lower overhead. Money stays fully liquid: deposit or withdraw anytime, no lock-up period.

Rate

Variable

The rate can change at any time, up or down, generally tracking broader interest rates (e.g. the Fed funds rate).

Liquidity

Fully liquid

Withdraw anytime, usually free, with no penalty — a common home for an emergency fund.

Backing

FDIC/NCUA insured

Up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank — see the "?" above.

Certificates of Deposit (CDs)

You lock up a set amount of money with a bank for a fixed term (e.g. 6 months, 1 year, 5 years) in exchange for a fixed interest rate for that whole term — the rate doesn't change even if broader rates do. Withdrawing early usually costs an early withdrawal penalty, often a few months of interest.

What's a "CD ladder"? (click to expand)

Instead of putting all your money into one CD term, you split it across several CDs with staggered end dates (e.g. 3, 6, 9, and 12 months). As each one matures you get access to part of your money regularly, and can reinvest it at whatever the current rate is — a common way to balance the better rate of a CD against the flexibility of not locking everything up at once.

Rate

Fixed for the term

Locked in the day you open it — a plus if rates fall later, a downside if they rise.

Liquidity

Locked until maturity

Early withdrawal generally forfeits some interest as a penalty.

Backing

FDIC/NCUA insured

Same $250,000-per-depositor protection as a savings account, at a bank/credit union.

Treasury bills (T-bills) T-bills are sold at a discount to their face value and pay the full face value at maturity — the difference is your interest. Example (illustrative, not a live rate): buy a $1,000 face-value bill for $980; get $1,000 back at maturity, a $20 return.

Short-term debt issued directly by the US government, with terms from 4 to 52 weeks. Considered among the safest investments that exist, since they're backed by the "full faith and credit" of the US government. Buy them directly (no fee) at TreasuryDirect.gov, or through most regular brokerage accounts.

Rate

Fixed, set at auction

Locked in for the short term you choose (weeks to a year).

Liquidity

Sellable before maturity

Can be sold on the secondary market early (via a brokerage), though the price you get can move with rates — unlike a bank CD's fixed penalty.

Backing & Taxes

US government backed

Interest is exempt from state and local income tax (still subject to federal tax) — a plus over a savings account or CD in high-tax states.

Saving vs. investing: single stocks and mutual funds

Once money is earmarked for something years away, some of it commonly moves from "safe and steady" (the options above) toward "grow over time, with more ups and downs" — investing in stocks or funds. See the full walkthrough on Get Started Investing.

Buying a single stock

You own a piece of one company. Can rise faster than the broader market — or fall much further, even to zero. Higher potential reward, higher concentration risk.

Buying a mutual fund / ETF

One purchase buys a slice of many companies at once. A fund like VOO holds roughly 500 large US companies, so one company's bad quarter barely moves it. See common index funds for more examples.

Which is "better"?

Neither, universally — funds are the more common beginner starting point for built-in diversification; single stocks let you concentrate on companies you've researched, with concentrated risk to match. Many people do both, with funds as the larger share.
