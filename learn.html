Get Started Investing A zero-background guide for total beginners — general education, not personalized advice

Educational only — not financial, tax, or legal advice. This page explains how investing generally works so unfamiliar terms stop being scary. It doesn't tell you what to buy.
New here? 60-second overview (click to expand)

Investing means putting money into something (like a stock or fund) hoping it grows in value over years — different from a savings account, which is for money you might need soon (see Money Management & Savings).

You can't just "buy a stock" out of thin air — you first open an account at a brokerage (a company that connects you to the stock market), put money into it, then use that money to buy. This page walks through that process and the account types you'll run into.

How to start — step by step

  1. Build a small emergency fund first. A cushion of cash (often ~3–6 months of expenses) in a regular or high-yield savings account, before investing money you might need on short notice. See Money Management & Savings.
  2. Get clear on your goal and timeline. Saving for retirement decades away? A house in 5 years? General long-term growth? The right account and how much risk makes sense both depend on this.
  3. Pick an account type. Retirement accounts (like a Roth IRA) get tax advantages but restrict withdrawals; a regular brokerage account is fully flexible but has no special tax treatment. See the comparison below.
  4. Choose a brokerage and open the account. Most major US brokerages are free to open, with no minimum. See the brokerage comparison on the Index Funds page.
  5. Fund the account. Link a bank account and transfer money in — this step alone doesn't buy anything yet, it just moves cash into the account.
  6. Choose what to buy. A broad, low-cost index fund (like one tracking the S&P 500) is a common beginner starting point — instantly diversified across hundreds of companies in one purchase, instead of betting on a single company. See common index funds.
  7. Automate it and leave it alone. Investing a fixed amount on a regular schedule (dollar-cost averaging) and staying invested through ups and downs is a common long-term approach — no need to time the market.

Which account should I use? The account is just the "container" your investments sit in — it determines the tax rules, not what you're allowed to buy. Figures below are general 2025 US guidelines; confirm current numbers with the IRS or your brokerage, and check eligibility for your situation (see the visa & tax section if you're on a visa).

Taxable · Fully Flexible

Brokerage account

A regular investing account with no contribution limits and no withdrawal restrictions — deposit or withdraw anytime. You pay tax each year on dividends and on gains when you sell. Good default if you might need the money before retirement, or you've maxed out retirement accounts.

Retirement · Tax-Free Growth

Roth IRA

You contribute money you've already paid tax on; it then grows and can be withdrawn tax-free in retirement. 2025 limit: $7,000/yr ($8,000 if 50+), and requires US earned income. Income limits apply at higher earnings. Contributions (not earnings) can generally be withdrawn early penalty-free.

Retirement · Tax Deferred

Traditional IRA

Contributions may reduce your taxable income now; the money is taxed later when withdrawn in retirement instead. Same 2025 contribution limit as a Roth ($7,000/yr, $8,000 if 50+). Early withdrawals before 59½ generally trigger a 10% penalty plus tax, with some exceptions.

Retirement · Employer Plan

401(k) / 403(b)

Offered through a job; contributions come straight out of your paycheck, before you ever see the money. Often includes an employer match — free money if you contribute enough to get it, commonly the first thing to prioritize before other investing. 2025 limit: $23,500/yr.

Bonus · Triple Tax Advantage

HSA (Health Savings Account)

Only available with a high-deductible health plan. Contributions are tax-deductible, growth is tax-free, and withdrawals are tax-free for medical expenses — often invested like a retirement account once a cash cushion is met.

Kids · Custodial

Custodial account (UTMA/UGMA)

Lets an adult invest on behalf of a minor; the minor legally owns it and gains full control at the age of majority (18–21, by state). No contribution limit, but no special tax break either — taxed similarly to a regular brokerage account, with some income taxed at the child's rate.

A common beginner order: get any full 401(k) employer match first, then a Roth or Traditional IRA, then a regular brokerage account for anything beyond that — but the right order depends on your income, tax situation, and goals.

Trading terms worth knowing

Market order

"Buy/sell right now, at whatever the current price is." Fills almost instantly but you don't control the exact price you get.

Limit order

"Only buy/sell at this price or better." You control the price, but the order might not fill at all if the market never reaches it.

Bid / ask spread

The bid is the highest price a buyer will pay right now; the ask is the lowest price a seller will accept. The small gap between them is the spread.

Single stock vs. fund

A single stock (e.g. one company) can swing wildly and even go to zero. A mutual fund or ETF (e.g. VOO) holds many companies at once, spreading out that risk. See fund types.

Dividend

A cash payment some companies/funds make to shareholders, usually quarterly, as a share of profits. Not guaranteed and can be reduced or stopped.

Volatility

How much a price swings up and down. Higher volatility means bigger, faster moves in both directions — more potential gain, more potential loss.

Common beginner mistakes

  1. Investing money you'll need soon. Prices can drop sharply in the short term; money needed within 1–3 years is usually kept out of the market.
  2. Putting it all in one stock. Exciting, but one bad company can sink a big chunk of your money — a broad fund spreads that risk out.
  3. Trying to time the market. Guessing the "perfect" moment to buy or sell is notoriously hard even for professionals; investing on a regular schedule sidesteps the guesswork.
  4. Panic selling during a drop. Locks in the loss. Markets have historically recovered over long periods, though past performance never guarantees future results.
  5. Ignoring fees. A high expense ratio or frequent trading costs quietly eat into returns over decades — see expense ratio.
  6. Skipping the emergency fund. Without one, an unexpected bill can force you to sell investments at a bad time. See Money Management & Savings.
