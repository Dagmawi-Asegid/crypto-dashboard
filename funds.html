Investing basics — plain English glossary

Click any term to expand it.

Stock

A tiny slice of ownership in one company. Price goes up when it does well, down when it doesn't — not guaranteed either way.

Index

A list that tracks a group of stocks together, like a scoreboard. "The S&P 500" = 500 large US companies. "The market went up" usually means an index like this went up.

Index fund

Buys a little bit of every company in an index for you, in one purchase — instant ownership in hundreds of companies at once, instead of picking stocks yourself.

Mutual fund vs. ETF

Both pool many investors' money into one fund. A mutual fund prices once a day. An ETF trades all day like a stock, through a regular brokerage. Most modern index funds are ETFs.

Diversification

Spreading money across many companies instead of one or two, so one bad company can't sink your whole investment. Index funds are diversified by design.

Expense ratio

The fund's annual fee, taken automatically as a % of your investment. Example: 0.03% on $1,000 ≈ $0.30/year. Lower is generally better.

Risk & volatility

How much a price swings. A single stock swings wildly; a broad index fund swings less since gains and losses across many companies partly cancel out. No return without some risk.

Dollar-cost averaging

Investing a fixed amount on a regular schedule (like every payday) instead of guessing the "perfect" time to buy. Removes the pressure of timing the market.

Compounding

Growth that earns more growth. Over years this snowballs — why "start early" and "stay invested long-term" get repeated so often.

Ticker symbol

A company/fund's short nickname, used to look it up or trade it: AAPL = Apple, VOO = Vanguard's S&P 500 fund.

Getting started — a common beginner framework Common financial-literacy framing repeated across many independent guides — not tailored to you. Your right choice depends on income, timeline, risk comfort, and tax situation, which this page can't know.

5 steps many guides repeat (click to expand)
  1. Emergency cash first. A cushion (often ~3–6 months of expenses) in savings, before investing money you might need soon.
  2. Only invest money you won't need for years. Prices can drop sharply short-term; money needed within 1–3 years is usually kept out of the market.
  3. A broad, low-cost index fund is a common starting point — instantly diversified, less single-company risk than picking stocks. No promise of returns.
  4. Investing on a regular schedule (dollar-cost averaging) is a common beginner approach — no need to time the market.
  5. This still isn't personal advice. A licensed advisor can apply this to your actual situation.

International students & visa holders

Short answer: yes, you can invest. Buying stocks/funds is investing, not employment — visa work-authorization rules don't apply to a brokerage account. What differs is taxes.
F-1 / OPT / CPT / J-1 / H-1B: what to know (click to expand)

🪪 Opening an account

Needs an SSN (from on-campus work, CPT, or OPT) or an ITIN, plus a US address. You'll also fill out Form W-8BEN — a routine form certifying your nonresident status so the broker withholds tax correctly.

🧾 Your tax status

F-1/J-1 students are usually treated as nonresident aliens for their first 5 calendar years (filing Form 1040-NR), which changes how investment income is taxed — see below.

📈 Capital gains & dividends

Gains from selling stock are generally untaxed only if you're in the US under 183 days/year — most full-time students exceed that, so a flat 30% federal tax can apply (unless your country's tax treaty reduces it). Dividends are withheld at 30% by default too, also reducible by treaty via Form W-8BEN.

🏦 IRAs

Roth/Traditional IRA contributions generally need US earned income (CPT/OPT or on-campus wages) — scholarships don't count. A regular brokerage account has no such rule.

🙋 Get real answers for your case

Treaty rates vary by country. Start with your school's international student office (ISSS) — many partner with Sprintax for exactly this.

Tech-Heavy

100 largest non-financial Nasdaq companies — historically tech-heavy, more volatile.

Bonds

BND AGG

International

Companies outside the US, for geographic diversification.

Dividend-Focused

VYM SCHD

Small-cap funds
